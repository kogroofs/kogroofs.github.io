After a hail or wind storm: what to check and what to do

Updated October 2026

The short answer

Stay off the roof. Check from the ground, take dated photos, stop any active leak, and report the damage to your insurance company. Get a written inspection, take your time before signing anything, and never accept an offer to cover your deductible.

Not sure the hail reached your street? Our free hail check shows the days NOAA radar detected hail near any address in Dallas-Fort Worth, and how big it was.

How to tell if your roof has hail damage

You can learn a lot from the ground, without climbing. Please stay off the roof. We'll get up there and photograph what we find.

Start on the ground

  • Soft metal. Dents in gutters, downspouts, roof vents, mailboxes, and the fins on your AC unit show that hail hit hard enough to matter, and which direction it came from. The size of the dents gives a rough idea of the size of the stones.
  • Spatter marks. Fresh round marks on painted wood, fences, and dirty or weathered metal, where hail knocked off the grime or paint.
  • Granules. More shingle grit than usual piled at the bottom of downspouts or sitting in the gutters.
  • Everything else. Torn window screens, cracked siding, dented patio furniture, and your car. If the car took hail, the roof did too.

What hail looks like on shingles

This is what we look for up close, on every slope:

  • Dark spots where the granules were knocked off, often with the black mat showing through.
  • Soft spots that give when you press them, like a bruise on an apple. The mat underneath is fractured.
  • Cracks or broken edges on the shingle.
  • A random pattern. Hail doesn't hit in lines.
  • More hits on the slopes that faced the storm than on the sheltered side.

What looks like hail but isn't

Adjusters are trained to tell these apart, and so are we. Knowing them can save you a claim that won't pay.

  • Blistering. Small raised bubbles that pop and leave pits. It comes from heat and trapped moisture, and a hot, poorly vented attic makes it worse. See why Texas roofs die early.
  • Normal granule loss. Older shingles shed granules evenly across the whole roof. Hail knocks them off in spots.
  • Scuffs and foot traffic. Marks in lines or paths, where someone walked or something was dragged.
  • Old cracks and nail pops. Weathered cracks with dirty, worn edges have been there a while. Fresh hail breaks look clean.
  • Tree limbs. Scrapes and gouges in one area, under a tree.

How an adjuster checks

Many adjusters mark a test square, often 10 feet by 10 feet, on each slope and count the hail hits inside it. That's why we photograph each slope separately and mark what we find, so your report and the adjuster are looking at the same thing.

Wind, flat roofs, metal, and inside

  • Wind. Look for missing shingles, shingles creased where they lifted and folded back, loose ridge caps, and debris on the roof.
  • Flat roofs. Hail can puncture or crack the membrane and dent the metal edge and coping. It often doesn't leak right away.
  • Metal roofs. Dents are common, and many are cosmetic. What matters is whether a seam, fastener, or panel was opened to water. Check your policy for a cosmetic damage exclusion.
  • Inside. Check ceilings, the attic, and around light fixtures for water stains.

The first 48 hours

  1. Stay safe. Keep clear of downed power lines, and stay off a wet or damaged roof.
  2. Take photos. Dated pictures of the roof from the ground, gutters, vents, siding, windows, and any leaks inside.
  3. Stop more damage. Put out buckets, move belongings, and tarp the roof if water is coming in. Keep the receipts; most policies expect you to take reasonable steps to prevent more damage. Water coming in now? Here's what to do right now.
  4. Report it. Call your insurance company or agent and ask for a claim number. Ask what your deductible is. Some policies have a separate wind and hail deductible. Don't wait: many policies set a deadline for reporting damage.
  5. Get an inspection. A written report with photos shows what was damaged and what it will take to fix.

How the inspection and claim work

A roofer can inspect the roof, document the damage, and give you a written report and estimate. You can share it with your insurance company. Your insurer sends an adjuster to inspect, and you can ask your roofer to be there to point out what they found.

The claim itself is between you and your insurance company. In Texas, a roofer who is doing the work can't act as your public adjuster or negotiate your claim. If you want someone to negotiate for you, Texas licenses public adjusters for that job.

For what decides whether insurance pays for a new roof, and what to do if your claim is denied, read Will insurance pay for a new roof after hail?

How to spot a storm chaser

  • Pressure to sign the same day, before your adjuster has even looked.
  • No local address, and a phone number or truck plates from out of state.
  • A request for full payment, or a large deposit, before any materials or work.
  • Any offer to pay, waive, or cover your deductible, or a "rebate" or "advertising allowance" that offsets it. That's illegal in Texas and several other states.
  • A contract that is mostly blank, or one that hands them control of your insurance claim.

Texas law also gives you three business days to cancel a contract you sign at your home, and the contractor has to give you a written cancellation form.
