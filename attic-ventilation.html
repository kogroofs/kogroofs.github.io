Why Texas roofs die early: attic ventilation

How your attic breathes, and what it does to your shingles. Updated October 2026.

The short answer

An attic has to breathe. Outside air should come in low, through vents in the soffits under the eaves, and hot air should go out high, through a vent at the ridge. When either end is blocked or missing, the attic holds heat and moisture, and the shingles cook from underneath. Most shingle makers require proper ventilation, and a roof without it can lose some or all of its warranty.

How an attic breathes

Hot air rises. On a summer afternoon the sun bakes the roof deck, and the attic under it gets far hotter than the air outside. A working attic puts that to use: hot air escapes at the top, and that draws cooler outside air in at the bottom. The air keeps moving as long as both ends are open.

It takes two parts, and they have to match:

  • Intake, down low. Vents in the soffits, the boards under the eaves. This is where fresh air comes in.
  • Exhaust, up high. A ridge vent along the peak, or vents near it. This is where the hot, wet air goes out.

GAF, the shingle maker, says the exhaust at the ridge should never be more than the intake at the soffits. An exhaust vent with nothing feeding it can't move much air, so it pulls from wherever it can.

Why Texas heat makes it matter

A shingle is mostly asphalt, and heat ages asphalt. When the attic can't shed its heat, the shingles take it from both sides: the sun on top and a hot attic underneath. GAF warns that trapped heat and moisture stress the shingles and the rest of the roof and can make them wear out early. With our summers, that adds up fast.

Moisture is the other half. Showers, cooking, and laundry put water in the air, and some of it rises into the attic. Without airflow it collects on the decking on cold nights. Over time that means rusty nails, dark stained decking, and mold.

What the building code asks for

Texas cities build homes to the International Residential Code. Its attic rule, section R806.2, calls for 1 square foot of open vent area for every 150 square feet of attic floor. That drops to 1 for every 300 when 40 to 50 percent of the vent area is up high, within 3 feet of the ridge, and the rest is down low at the eaves.

So a 2,000-square-foot attic, balanced that way, needs about 6.7 square feet of open vent area, split between the soffits and the ridge. "Open" means the actual openings, called net free area, which is printed on the vent's label. It's smaller than the vent looks.

What we find most often

  • Soffits with no vents, or vents that are blocked. Solid soffit boards, vents painted shut, or attic insulation packed into the eaves until no air can get past it.
  • A ridge vent with no intake to feed it. It looks right from the street and does very little.
  • Mixed exhaust vents. A ridge vent plus turbines, box vents, or a power fan on the same attic. Gibraltar Building Products, which makes Air Vent attic vents, says it's never OK to mix two types of exhaust on one attic. One of them can turn into an intake and short-circuit the airflow, which leaves parts of the attic dead and can let weather in.
  • Bathroom fans blowing into the attic. They should go outside through their own vent. Otherwise every shower sends steam up onto the decking.

What we look for in your attic

On an inspection, we get in the attic when it's needed. Here's what we check:

  • Daylight at the soffits, and whether insulation is blocking them
  • What kind of exhaust is on the roof, and whether types are mixed
  • Whether the intake and exhaust are roughly balanced for the size of the attic
  • Rusty nails, stained or soft decking, and signs of mold
  • Where the bathroom and kitchen fans actually end up

What we find goes in your written report, with photos.

How it gets fixed

The best time is during a roof replacement, when the old vents are coming off anyway. We cut the ridge open and install a ridge vent, remove the old box vents or turbines and patch the deck so the exhaust isn't mixed, and make sure the soffits can feed it.

Some roofs, like hip roofs with a short ridge, don't have enough ridge for a ridge vent to do the job alone. On those we use vents set high on the roof instead, all of one type.

On a roof that isn't being replaced, soffit vents and baffles that hold the insulation back can be added from below without touching the shingles. Ventilation work is listed and priced in your written estimate, so you can see what it costs and why it's there.

Common questions

Will better ventilation cool my house?

It can take some load off your air conditioner, because a cooler attic puts less heat on the ceiling. But insulation and sealing gaps in the ceiling usually do more for your power bill. Ventilation is mostly about protecting the roof.

Should I add a power attic fan?

Usually not. A power fan shouldn't be mixed with a ridge vent, and without enough intake it can pull air up out of the house through gaps in the ceiling: cooled air you paid for. Get the intake and exhaust right first.

Can a badly vented attic void my shingle warranty?

It can. Most shingle makers require proper attic ventilation, and without it the warranty is often voided or cut shorter. Ask any roofer how they're handling ventilation before you sign.

My roof has turbines. Are they bad?

Not by themselves. Turbines can work as the only exhaust on an attic with enough soffit intake. The trouble starts when they share an attic with a ridge vent or box vents.
